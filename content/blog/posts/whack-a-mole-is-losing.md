I want to juxtapose two classes of people in the security industry on the defender side of [security engineering for products][security-engineering-for-products]. The first are the Security as Identity people. This goes back to the security-as-counterculture attitude, glamorized in Hackers and dating back to phone phreaking. It reflects Thompson’s "[Workism][workism]", where work is not just a way to earn a living, but a way to build a sense of identity, purpose, and community. In the case of security, this identity tends to be built around being different, seeing things other people do not, and being clever in an edgy and misunderstood way. Someone who is beyond merely a security professional, but has the persona of a Hacker. Stylish outsiders smarter than the system. Detectives finding insights others miss.

There’s nothing wrong with the hacker ethos. The idea of finding places where systems break down and work in unexpected or incorrect ways is obviously extremely valuable. Careers, conferences, and professional communities all reward vulnerability discovery, the more surprising the better. Similarly, the hacker aesthetic is what it is. At this point, it’s mainstream. Around 25,000 people attend DEF CON each year.

Valuing unexpected insights is not only a hacker thing. In the 2000s, the book _[Freakonomics][freakonomics]_ was extremely popular. It also took the form of proposing surprising explanations for seemingly straightforward or otherwise unremarkable social phenomena. If you can find something unexpected, you must be very smart and cool! But should surprise really be a signal of intelligence? You can take that attitude even farther, and do a [Paul Graham style "it turns out" misdirection][it-turns-out]. Instead of saying you believe in Y, say you expected X, investigated, and it turns out that Y is true. That way, you don’t have to justify Y, you just say X is false.

The identity you choose matters beyond aesthetics. Problem definitions inherently carry an associated worldview, and it’s very difficult to change a worldview, particularly if you’ve built your identity, community, and profession around that worldview. Evidence for solutions that address the underlying problem, but not in a way that validates the worldview, can be rejected. As a concrete example, Nordhaus and Shellenberger address this failure mode for climate change activism in their notorious "[Death of Environmentalism][death-of-environmentalism]" essay from 2004. Environmentalists wanted to get people to agree to protect "the environment" as a supposed "thing", a special interest deserving of good, that should have technical policy interventions in order to advance it, specifically. This meant solutions needed to take the form of being related to "the environment", like cap-and-trade and carbon emission limitations. Solutions that resulted in the desired outcome of less greenhouse gases, but were not sufficiently "environmental", such as decreasing clean energy costs (even with increased usage), higher-yield agriculture (not just organic farm-to-table), and the idea that technological substitution can work better than behavioral modification, were rejected. Ironically, in the two decades since the controversial essay, we can see that many of these rejected solutions are the ones ultimately played out. Improvements in solar and battery technology, alongside electric cars and other renewables, many of which had government subsidies in the 2010s, did more to reduce greenhouse emissions than the policies that validated the accepted worldview of environmentalism in the 2000s.

In security, solutions that do not look like repeatedly discovering, exploiting, and patching technically impressive vulnerabilities may be overlooked if they do not satisfy the worldview of the Security as Identity person, particularly if they would rather act as a detective than as a mechanic. An attacker mindset and a coherent threat model are important to building secure systems. But while necessary, are they complete? And are they even a good starting point? We do not teach people to cook by telling them to "think like a chef".

This brings us to the second class of person, the Security as Robustness people. It turns out, some people in security are primarily motivated by building correct systems that preserve their desired properties, including during failure conditions, edge cases, and inconsistencies. Security is part of building a well-engineered system that operates properly in all conditions, including in the face of an attacker.

To illustrate the difference, let’s look at how people think about vulnerability rewards programs (VRPs), also known as bug bounties. What’s the point of a VRP? At minimum, a VRP serves as a point of contact for security issues such that they can be patched in product before they are leveraged by attackers. If you run a sufficiently large product (or, in particular, a platform), you will have people contact you with security issues. At least some of the security reports will be valid, and require patches. A VRP is a standard process for this that forces the organization to have the ability to respond to both external and internal security bug reports, filed by people other than the individual feature teams working on some part of the product. It financially incentivizes participants to report bugs to you in a way that’s productive for you. There’s a very straightforward economic argument to say the VRP is successful so long as the number of bugs found increases faster than the relative payout prices. The more bugs found by the VRP, the better.

Beyond bug finding and patching, VRPs can and should be used to inform the strategy of the security team. The VRP is as close to a replacement for the role of the customer in the standard approach to product management as you can get. Attackers aren’t your users, and the full set of insecurity is an unknown unknown. While it won’t enumerate the entire attack surface, the VRP is a repeated and consistent way to gain insight into attacker methodologies and validate the work of the security team, such that you can bound the size and impact of the unknown unknowns. What types of bugs are showing up in the VRP, where in the product are they occurring, why are they happening, and what teams own them can all be inputs to a security roadmap that should be [building towards a future where the bugs are rare][security-teams-are-not-a-lottery-ticket]. VRPs are also a two-way street. Vulnerability researchers react to incentives, so if the security team wants to validate a hypothesis to justify some security initiative, they can also tweak the rules and rewards in the VRP to incentivize submissions that either validate the hypothesis and can be used as collateral to justify the roadmap to other teams and leadership, or that show the hypothesis was incorrect. If the VRP results suggest the roadmap is not attached to reality, the security team can reject the hypothesis and try something different. One of the best things about a VRP is that what bug hunters look for is not constrained by the beliefs of the people who work on the product.

When the VRP is used as an input to a broader strategy, the overarching goal of a VRP is not to fully enumerate all bugs and thus have a secure product. The point of a VRP is to grapple with the insecurity. Arguably, dealing with bugs that come externally through the VRP is the worst possible way to prioritize those bugs, because it is inherently interrupt-driven and not capacity-managed by the teams that patch the bugs. Patching VRP bugs is another case of playing whack-a-mole. Actually securing something remains the responsibility of the security team, who should be making sure that either the bugs can’t happen (e.g. via safe coding), or that the bugs don’t matter anymore (e.g. through architectural changes that lower impact). This distinction with VRPs, between enumerating all bugs versus informing a strategy to reduce insecurity over time, is the litmus test for Security as Robustness.

There’s a polarity in that finding more issues even quicker is good, because it is better to find things and patch them than to never know there were problems, but it is also bad because it is evidence that your system is not working as intended. Finding problems is a diagnostic metric. It proves that your sensor is working. Tracking incidence of types of problems is an outcome metric. Ensuring that the same type of problem is not happening repeatedly over and over again is the actual sign of success.

Every bug or incident is a failure, and while failures do happen, we should be mad about it if they are happening all the time, even if we are responding to them very fast. Outside of security, consider site reliability engineering (SRE). If a service is consistently having downtime or other errors that violate the SLO and consume the error budget eventually, an SRE team, [under the original definition of the term][sre-book], is empowered to halt feature development and releases, move resources towards reliability rather than features, and work with the product team to build and deploy systemic fixes. Once SLO compliance is back within the error budget, product work continues. In other words, the primary long-term goal is robustness, quickly resolving SLO non-compliance incidents is secondary. The SRE approach is not whack-a-mole, but it does require alignment at the leadership level for when the SRE team is allowed to intervene and potentially interrupt the goals of the product team.

Unfortunately, it is far too easy in security for whack-a-mole to become the goal. It’s great at the metrics and compliance game because you can make a graph of resolved incidents going up and to the right. Maybe even time-to-resolution going down over time! It also requires less organizational alignment than intervening in the roadmaps of other teams, making it easy to have the appearance of a security team that owns its own destiny. Taking ownership of actual security outcomes is scary, because attackers are unknown. But playing whack-a-mole is losing.

Every mole that is whacked is an opportunity for an attacker. Optimizing for reacting to issues, only to resolve them quickly (and in some cases, whack the same [hole][the-hole-rce] [over][chromium-hole-1] and [over][chromium-hole-2] and [over][chromium-hole-3] [again][chromium-hole-search]) can make it feel like you’re succeeding, when you’re actually losing. Winning is not having issues in the first place. The only thing better than a graph of resolved incidents over time that goes up and to the right, is a graph of incidence of some bug class over time that goes down and to the right, approaching zero. Recognizing that finding and patching vulnerabilities is a skill that a security team needs, but not a good north star, can be scary if your career and worldview are built around security being finding clever bugs that other people can’t see. This can lead to tension where, if you have built your identity around the problems, then any solution starts to look like an attack on your identity. The reason why you got into security will often impact how you think about the problem and, as Nordhaus said, your worldview and solutions will reflect that. Are you in security because you like finding unexpected insights and solving puzzles, or because you want to build systems and security is one of the most interesting places to build them?

The attitude that security is a counterculture about unexpected insights is prevalent enough that even if you haven’t built your identity around it, people who are not primarily working on security think a bunch of genius hackers is what it means to have a successful security team. Unfortunately, if your perception of security is that it is about searching for insecurity, it seems unlikely that you’ll ever build a secure system. You’ll just keep playing whack-a-mole until eventually you collapse under your own weight.

The hacker mindset may help you understand how a system fails, and if that failure can be leveraged for nefarious means, but security is more than finding the insecurity. A good security team is thinking critically about underlying causes and systemic fixes, and wants to win by building a system that is fundamentally better than before. Beyond articulating attacker capabilities and threat models and then searching an effectively unbounded search space for problems, more defenders should define invariants about what should be true about a system. Breaking these invariants is bad whether or not an attacker does it. Good security teams are looking for deviations from good, rather than trying to search for the bad. In many cases, these invariants can be implemented in code through languages, interfaces, and safe abstractions, and enforced in CI using tools [as simple as grep][devd-grep]. Other invariants may be enforced through architecture and operational controls. A vulnerability is evidence that some invariant is missing, wrong, or unenforced. Success means continually updating and encoding these invariants so that entire classes of vulnerability stop recurring.

Verifiable properties of a system are needed even more in the age of AI agents. Vulnerability discovery is currently the cheapest it has ever been. Agents also help with remediation, although not as effectively as at vulnerability discovery. At first glance, if AI is making discovery and remediation cheaper than ever before, perhaps a reactive approach can finally scale? But even with AI, whack-a-mole is still losing. A cheaper diagnostic does not inherently change the outcome. If you can always find another bug, the marginal benefit of patching the next one is zero.

The [conventional wisdom][argv-tweet] around the "[vulnpocalypse][scw-lcamtuf]" is that we’re in a local minimum, where security debt from the last 30 years is all being called in at once. Once we get through patching that, eventually, software will be much more secure overall and the expected cost of finding and exploiting a vulnerability will go up.

This will only be true if we take an active role in actually improving things. A better future does not simply happen, you have to bring it into existence. Things do not get better without effort, and in this instance, [Jevons paradox][jevons-paradox] is fighting to maintain the status quo. Using agents, you can choose to simply [play whack-a-mole even faster][dft-wired]. At the same time, software engineers are using agents to [write exponentially more code][github-august-17-outage]. While the token cost of "scanning" for vulnerabilities will go down over time, the complexity and costs of doing so will go up alongside the lines of code, which are increasing exponentially, suggesting that AI vulnerability hunting will have the same shortcomings as fuzzing for defenders. After the initial set of vulnerabilities pop out, it’s exponential costs for defenders to attempt to enumerate all vulnerabilities, but only polynomial costs for attackers to find useful vulnerabilities. For a sufficiently large project, solely using AI to find vulnerabilities will never result in a future where vulnerabilities are rare.

This doesn’t have to happen! Defenders also have coding agents and vulnerability hunting agents. Now is the time to leverage them to identify and address systemic issues that allow you to prevent vulnerabilities by construction. [You cannot bug fix your way out of the vulnpocalypse][gaynor-vulnpocalypse]. We only get the good outcome if we choose to make things better, instead of choosing to play more whack-a-mole. For AI to scale, defenders need to be able to articulate the invariants of the system, and then leverage AI to implement verifiable enforcement of the invariants. This also helps the non-security engineers using AI, who will have more verifiable outcomes for their own coding agents, enabling more productive agent-driven development.

Producing an endless stream of findings is not a good use of security expertise, human or AI. If all you do is play whack-a-mole, you’re going to lose. We should not view security as an endless supply of mysteries to solve because being a detective is the valued identity. Instead, the role of defenders is to turn failures into new, broader, and stronger invariants, and to encode these invariants into the system so that engineers (and agents) don’t have to constantly rediscover the same class of problems. If that outcome feels like a loss, it’s because identity has displaced security as the goal.
